# CAFE: an open startup investment agreement in which the investment is not debt


*Translated from the Portuguese original. The CAFE template is drafted under Brazilian law.*

CAFE, short for Contrato de Adiantamento para Futura Emissão (Advance for Future Issuance Agreement), is an open startup investment agreement template. It does not create debt: the investment is irrevocable and allocated to the company's share capital. Each party's ownership is calculated transparently. The template is available to founders, investors and lawyers. This article explains what makes CAFE different and the reasons behind each choice.

**IN SHORT**
- The convertible loan (mútuo conversível), one of the most common instruments in Brazil, treats an investment in a startup as a loan.
- CAFE is not a loan: the investment bears no interest and converts into equity.
- The investor's stake results from a formula, even when the investment exceeds the share capital.
- Before signing, everyone who will be diluted sees the cap table and accepts the dilution.
- The agreement and the cap table calculator are free, under the CC BY 4.0 license.

## The mismatch between risk and contract

Whoever invests in an early-stage startup accepts the risk of losing everything. That is the venture capital bargain: the investor bets on the company's growth and, if the business succeeds, receives equity.

In Brazil, however, this bargain is often documented as a debt agreement. The convertible loan, one of the most widely used instruments in the market, treats the investment as a loan that may be converted into equity.

> The contract says there is debt where the parties agreed on risk.

This mismatch has a cost. It may trigger IOF (Brazil's tax on financial transactions), opens the door to collecting the amount as a credit when the business does not succeed, and fuels litigation over the true nature of the transaction. The startup's balance sheet shows a debt that nobody intended to collect.

This risk has already reached the courts. In 2021, the São Paulo State Court of Appeals (TJSP) upheld a payment order lawsuit (ação monitória) that sought to collect from a startup the amount it had received under a convertible loan. The investor chose not to convert, and the court held that it was a true loan, not a capital contribution, relying in part on the startup's own trial balance and an e-mail it had sent.

There is a second, less discussed problem: opacity. On signing day, neither the founder nor the investor can often say how much of the company each will hold after conversion. The question usually appears only in the next round, when it is too late to negotiate.

## The legal basis already exists

Brazilian law already allows an investment instrument that is not debt. Three statutes support this conclusion:

- **Complementary Law 182/2021, the Brazilian Startup Legal Framework (Marco Legal das Startups).** It allows investments that may or may not result in equity (art. 5) and leaves the list of instruments open. It shields the investor from management, voting and the startup's debts, except in cases of willful misconduct, fraud or simulation in which the investor took part (art. 8).
- **Law 10,406/2002, the Brazilian Civil Code.** It allows atypical contracts (art. 425), establishes minimal state intervention in contracts (art. 421, sole paragraph) and presumes business contracts to be negotiated between equals (art. 421-A).
- **Law 13,874/2019, the Economic Freedom Law (Lei da Liberdade Econômica).** It guarantees freedom of contract in business transactions between equals, subject to public policy rules (art. 3, VIII).

There is no lack of law. What is missing is an instrument that uses what the law already allows, with clauses clear enough to narrow the room for interpretation and, with it, the room for litigation.

## SAFE meets AFAC: an investment that is not debt

CAFE is an atypical contract based on these three statutes. Its goal is to give security to both sides: the startup receives the investment without taking on debt, and the investor is guaranteed the negotiated stake.

To do so, it combines two approaches. From the American SAFE, it takes conversion with a valuation cap and a discount. From the Brazilian advance for future capital increase (AFAC, its Portuguese acronym), it takes the nature of the investment: irrevocable and allocated to share capital.

The result is written into the agreement:
- **The investment is not credit.** It is not a loan or any other credit transaction, regardless of its accounting treatment.
- **No financial charges.** The investment bears no interest, inflation adjustment or any other return.
- **The investment is irrevocable.** The investor cannot withdraw the investment or demand its return, except in case of a serious breach by the startup or failure to pay the investment itself.
- **Its destination is share capital.** The startup records the amount in an advance for future capital increase account, and the investor becomes a shareholder only upon conversion.

Side by side, the difference from the convertible loan looks like this:

| | Convertible loan | CAFE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Loan that may be converted into equity | Advance allocated to share capital |
| Interest | May be provided for | Bears no interest |
| If the business does not succeed | The amount may be collected as a credit | The investment is irrevocable |
| Each party's stake | Usually becomes clear only in the next round | Shown in the cap table before signing |

*General comparison. Each loan agreement may have its own clauses.*

## A stake guaranteed by formula, even above the share capital

Under CAFE, no shareholder sells quotas or shares to the investor. The startup issues new quotas or shares to accommodate the investor's stake, and the entire investment stays in the company's cash. The dilution of the others comes from this issuance, not from a sale.

On this point, CAFE comes close to the subscription option, also provided for in the Startup Legal Framework, but is not the same. The main difference lies in payment. In an option, there are usually two payments with different purposes: the first buys the right, and the second pays for its exercise. If the investor walks away, what was paid for the right is not returned, and the investor gets no stake. Under CAFE, the whole investment, paid upfront or in installments as negotiated, is already the price of the new quotas or shares, with no second payment for conversion. The startup does not depend on the exercise of an option to receive the negotiated funds.

In many startups, the share capital is small compared with the investment. A limited liability company (LTDA) with share capital of BRL 100,000 may receive BRL 1 million from an investor. At conversion, two questions arise: how many quotas does that amount buy, and where does the difference go?

Without a clear rule, the answer is left to negotiation at the worst possible moment, when the startup has already grown and every percentage point is worth more.

CAFE answers in advance. In conversion at the cap, the investor's number of quotas or shares results from a formula that guarantees the negotiated percentage of the share capital after conversion:

**THE CONVERSION AT THE CAP FORMULA**

N = (P × C) ÷ (1 − P)

N is the investor's number of quotas or shares. P is the negotiated percentage, which results from the investment divided by the valuation cap. C is the total number of quotas or shares before conversion, already including other investors and vesting beneficiaries.

**EXAMPLE**

A startup has 100,000 quotas, divided among three shareholders. The investor negotiated 20%.

| | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shareholder A | 60,000 (60%) | 60,000 (48%) |
| Shareholder B | 20,000 (20%) | 20,000 (16%) |
| Shareholder C | 20,000 (20%) | 20,000 (16%) |
| Investor | — | 25,000 (20%) |
| Total | 100,000 | 125,000 |

No shareholder sells quotas: each keeps the same number. The startup issues 25,000 new quotas, the number needed for the investor to hold 20%: N = (0.20 × 100,000) ÷ (1 − 0.20) = 25,000.

If each quota is worth BRL 1 and the investment was BRL 1 million, with a valuation cap of BRL 5 million, BRL 25,000 goes to share capital and BRL 975,000 to the capital reserve.

Only the portion of the investment that corresponds to the value of the new quotas or shares goes to share capital. The excess goes to the capital reserve and does not give the investor additional quotas or shares. No later valuation changes this number.

The capital reserve is grounded in the Brazilian Corporations Law (Lei das S/A). The subscriber's contribution exceeding the par value of the shares forms a capital reserve (art. 13, § 2, and art. 182, § 1, a). For shares without par value, the law also allows part of the issue price to be allocated to the reserve (art. 14, sole paragraph).

For limited liability companies, the path runs through the Civil Code, which allows the articles of association to provide for the supplementary application of the Corporations Law (art. 1,053, sole paragraph). For this reason, the template recommends including this provision in the articles of association and, upon conversion, confirming the recording of the reserve with the accountant and the Commercial Registry (Junta Comercial).

## Whoever will be diluted signs first

The market's second problem is opacity. Those who invested earlier through a convertible instrument and those entitled to quotas under vesting are usually left out of the new investor's agreement. The dilution math appears only in the next round, and that is when disputes arise.

Under CAFE, everyone who will be diluted is a party to the agreement:
- **The shareholders**, all those listed in the articles of association of the LTDA or in the share register of the corporation (S/A), sign as intervening parties and are jointly and severally liable with the startup for its obligations.
- **Other Investors**, who invested earlier through a convertible instrument, and **Vesting Beneficiaries** also sign as intervening parties, but without joint and several liability, because they are not yet shareholders.

Everyone appears in a schedule attached to the agreement, the cap table, showing current ownership and ownership after conversion. The startup and its shareholders declare that the schedule is complete and correct.

In addition, everyone signs a waiver of preemptive rights and accepts proportional dilution, which affects everyone to the same extent, except those who invest in the same round. At each conversion, the startup sends the investor the updated cap table and the calculation report, which the investor may challenge.

To fill in the schedule, the template comes with a spreadsheet calculator. Just enter the investment amount, the negotiated cap and each person's quotas or shares. The spreadsheet applies the agreement's formula and shows the result, with no technical knowledge required.

**IMPORTANT**
The calculator does not calculate valuation. The company's value is a business decision of the parties. CAFE simply shows, clearly, the effect of that decision on each party's stake.

## An agreement meant to be read by those who sign it

Investment agreements are usually written for lawyers. CAFE was written for founders and investors.

> Legal certainty begins when those who sign understand what they are signing.

For this reason, the template applies Legal Design to what is usually a startup's densest document:
- one page that presents the entire agreement;
- an "In short" box, in plain language, at the start of each clause;
- visual highlights for passages that limit rights or impose penalties;
- schedules with numerical examples.

Technical guidance for lawyers and accountants is in a separate note at the end of the document.

**AND THE REST OF THE AGREEMENT?**
CAFE also includes the clauses expected in a good investment agreement, such as a use of proceeds plan, conversion upon request, investor exits, penalties, regulatory compliance and dispute resolution. Each one is explained in the template itself, with its own "In short" box.

## Why an open template?

The agreement, in Word and PDF, and the calculator are available for free, with no sign-up, on GitHub: github.com/GuilhermeAdv/cafe-modelo-aberto

Openness has a practical reason. Standard agreements gain security only when they are used, criticized and tested by many people. The versions are kept in a public GitHub repository, with a record of changes and credit for relevant contributions.

CAFE is a starting point, not a ready-made agreement. The original is in Portuguese and governed by Brazilian law. Each transaction calls for review by a lawyer and an accountant. Criticism and suggestions are welcome: that is how an open template matures and how the ecosystem gains an instrument that matches the risk it is willing to take.
